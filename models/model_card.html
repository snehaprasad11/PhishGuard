PhishGuard URL model

Version url-v0.4.0 · generated automatically from the evaluation run

What this model is

A logistic regression over 33 hand-engineered URL features, with Platt-scaled probabilities. It looks only at the text of a URL and whether its host is a well-known site — never at page content — and is an aid to judgement, not a guarantee.

Known limitations

  • The model trusts 9,889 well-known domains (Tranco top 10,000, minus shared-hosting platforms) more than other sites, except 72 hosts on them that were repeatedly used for phishing in the training period. 8.3% of recent PhishTank URLs are on the still-trusted hosts and are harder to catch. See the well-known-site rows above.
  • Benign training URLs are mostly deep pages from a web crawl while phishing URLs are mostly short landing pages, so results differ by URL shape. See the table above.
  • The benign sample cannot be filtered by page status, so it includes some error pages and redirects on legitimate sites.
  • Phishing hosted on legitimate platforms (form builders, site builders, URL shorteners) cannot be told apart from benign pages on the same platform by the URL alone.
  • Benign URLs from domains Tranco does not list are sampled from a web crawl without a reputation filter, so a small share of them may not be benign.
  • Phishing changes continuously; performance degrades between retrains.

PhishGuard text model

Version text-v0.1.0

A logistic regression over engineered signals in the wording of a pasted message: pressure to act, threats, requests for secrets, prizes, generic greetings. It is applied to pasted messages only, never to page content.

Data

SMS: UCI SMS Spam Collection. Phishing email: Nazario phishing corpus (CC-BY-4.0), split by year. Legitimate email: SpamAssassin public corpus. Near-identical messages are reduced to one, so no template is in two splits.

Why not a bag-of-words model

A TF-IDF bag-of-words model was trained on the same data for comparison. On the test split it is far stronger: PR-AUC 0.995, recall at 1% false positives 96.2%. But the test split comes from the same corpora as the training data, and the legitimate email in them is mailing-list traffic from the early 2000s. On ordinary modern messages written for this project (one-time codes, delivery updates, bank notices) it flags many legitimate ones:

Hand-written messagesShipped: legitimate flaggedShipped: scams caughtBag of words: legitimate flaggedBag of words: scams caught
Held-out list (never used for design)0 of 2011 of 1211 of 2012 of 12
Development list (guided the design)1 of 2514 of 1510 of 2515 of 15

Words the bag-of-words model treats as most legitimate: my, url, url _url_, it, list, _url_ date, me, that, perl, ok, lt gt, at, i'm, then, spamassassin. These are quirks of the corpus, not signs of a safe message.

Known limitations of the text model

  • It catches well under half of the scam messages in its own test split. It is a second opinion on wording, not a filter.
  • It only knows English phrases, and only the ones in its lists.
  • The hand-written lists are small, so their counts are indicative, not precise.
  • Its legitimate training email is two decades old.
